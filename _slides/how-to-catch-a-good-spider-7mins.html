However, implementing this algorithm computationally requires a system capable of distinguishing valid homotopic paths from tangled, intersecting ones. In this talk, we explore a computational framework that translates the continuous topological constraints of complex dynamics into discrete graph-theoretic algorithms. We will outline the process of generating discretized paths (spider legs) from a chosen base point to the post-critical set of a rational map, utilizing projective coordinates and the chordal metric to seamlessly handle limits at infinity. We then introduce an algorithmic method to evaluate the resulting monodromy graphs. By searching for vertex-disjoint paths and applying cyclic ordering, our algorithm programmatically verifies the existence of a "good spider" without human visual intervention. From PCF dynamics to a finite-state problem

Let $f$ be a rational map and $$P_f=\bigcup_{n\geq 1} f^n(C_f)$$ its post-critical set. When $P_f$ is finite, $f$ is post-critically finite (PCF).

Can we turn the topology of $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb C)\setminus P_f$ into an explicit finite automaton?
0:35. Give only the minimum background: critical points have finite forward orbits, so the punctured sphere has finitely many punctures. The goal is not to classify PCF maps here; it is to compute their monodromy.

Preimages form a rooted tree

Choose a base point $t\notin P_f$ and repeatedly take preimages:

$t \leftarrow f^{-1}(t) \leftarrow f^{-2}(t) \leftarrow \cdots$

Loops in $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb C)\setminus P_f$ permute this tree by path lifting.

$$\operatorname{IMG}(f)=\operatorname{im}\!\left(\pi_1(\mathbb P^1\setminus P_f,t)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(X^*)\right).$$

0:35. The iterated monodromy group records how loops permute all iterated preimages. Emphasize the picture rather than the formal construction.

A spider gives generators

A spider $\mathcal S=\{l_p\}_{p\in P_f}$ is a collection of non-crossing legs from $t$ to the points of $P_f$.

Travel out along $l_p$, circle $p$, and return: these loops generate the fundamental group.

0:35. A spider is a geometric coordinate system for the fundamental group. Each leg determines a standard loop around one post-critical point.

The computational obstruction

After lifting a generator $g$, we must recognize the section $$g'=\rho_x\,f^{-1}(g)[x]\,\rho_y^{-1}.$$

Is $g'$ homotopic to one of our standard spider loops—without asking a human to inspect a tangled picture?
  • continuous paths must be compared up to homotopy;
  • the paths must avoid the post-critical set and one another;
  • the point at infinity must be handled uniformly.
0:40. This is the bottleneck. Path lifting is straightforward; recognizing the resulting loop is not. The goal is to replace visual judgment with a discrete certificate.

Replace topology by a web

Pull the spider back under $f$:

$$G=f^{-1}(\mathcal S).$$

The resulting embedded graph records:

  • the preimages $f^{-1}(t)$;
  • the preimages of post-critical points;
  • the cyclic order of edges around every vertex.

A homotopy problem has become a graph-search problem.

0:45. The key move is discretization. Projective coordinates and the chordal metric let the same construction work near infinity. The embedding, especially cyclic order, retains the topological information we need.

How the search works

1
Discretize and lift the spider
2
Build the labelled web
3
Search for compatible, vertex-disjoint paths
4
Check cyclic order and local pole conditions

Output: a combinatorial certificate that the selected paths form a good spider.

0:50. Explain that the program generates candidate paths from one chosen head to the other preimages. Vertex-disjointness prevents crossings; cyclic ordering distinguishes paths that look identical as abstract graphs but differ in the plane. The local conditions certify the required homotopies.

A checkable sufficient criterion

Good-spider criterion

Let $z_0\in f^{-1}(t)$ be the head. Paths $\{\rho_x\}$ in the web form a good spider if:

  1. the paths from $z_0$ to the other points of $f^{-1}(t)$ are mutually compatible in the embedded web;
  2. $z_0$ is adjacent to at most two post-critical vertices, with no spider leg between them in cyclic order;
  3. at each endpoint $x$, exactly one unused neighboring vertex is post-critical.

These are finite, local conditions—so the verification can be automated.

0:55. State this as a sufficient criterion, not a complete characterization. The point is that global homotopy recognition is reduced to path compatibility plus local adjacency and cyclic-order checks.
